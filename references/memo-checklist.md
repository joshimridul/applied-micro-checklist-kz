# Source-derived checklist

**Source:** Ekaterina Zhuravskaya, *Rules for Good Academic Writing in Applied Economics: A Checklist of Dos and Don’ts*, the eight-page memo included in this package. The [original memo](original-memo.pdf) is included as supplied. No publication date is asserted.

This checklist paraphrases the memo in its original twelve-section order. It preserves the DO/DON’T distinction where the source uses it, the numerical targets, and the author's explicitly marked preferences. Rule IDs are added for navigation and audits; they are not in the original. Repeated rules are retained or cross-referenced.

The source's scope is **form, not research content, in applied microeconomics papers**. This checklist contains no rules from another writing guide.

## 1. General Principles

Source: §1, [page 1](original-memo.pdf#page=1).

- **M01.01 — One story.** Never put two great ideas in one paper; two focused papers serve readers better than one cluttered paper.
- **M01.02 — Narrative.** Make the argument hold together from introduction to conclusion; a paper is not a collection of results.
- **M01.03 — Spoken clarity.** Use formulations that make sense when read aloud.
- **M01.04 — Learn conventions.** Writing is a learnable craft; there is no need to invent a new style. Master existing conventions and imitate effective well-published papers without plagiarizing.
- **M01.05 — Claims and limits.** Do not overclaim. State limitations and alternative explanations explicitly.
- **M01.06 — First-time reader.** Always put yourself in the reader's shoes. If something is not clear to a reader encountering the paper for the first time, it is not clear.
- **M01.07 — Complete proofreading.** Recheck every detail, down to the last letter in the last appendix exhibit. Sloppiness even in an unimportant note signals sloppiness everywhere.
- **M01.08 — Care in English.** Correct poor English and typos. Readers, including editors and referees, take them as signals of low quality and a lack of care beyond language.

## 2. Paper Structure

Source: §2, [page 2](original-memo.pdf#page=2).

### DOs

- **M02.01 — Standard sequence.** Follow the standard structure: Introduction → Background → Theoretical framework, if not obvious → Data → Empirical methodology, including identification assumptions and tests → Results, including main results, robustness, and mechanisms → Conclusions → Figures → Tables → Appendix.
- **M02.02 — Logical purpose.** Each section should follow from the previous one; each paragraph and sentence should serve a clear purpose.
- **M02.03 — Deliberate departures.** Depart from the standard sequence deliberately. The memo gives combining methods and results for tests requiring different methods, and placing a model before or after results according to its prominence, as examples.
- **M02.04 — Organized appendix.** Use clearly labeled appendix sections, not an unorganized collection of leftover exhibits.
- **M02.05 — Explicit author preference: exhibits at the end.** Zhuravskaya prefers tables and figures at the end because this lets editors and referees flip through them and grasp the paper's main story before reading closely. She acknowledges opposing preferences and cites a journal exception. This is explicitly a preference, not a universal placement requirement.

### DON’Ts

- **M02.06 — No circular exposition.** Do not repeat yourself. Repetition is not emphasis; it is noise. Circling back loses editors and referees.
- **M02.07 — Different formulations.** Do not repeat the same formulations across the abstract, introduction, and conclusion, even when summarizing the same finding.
- **M02.08 — Paragraph length.** Do not write paragraphs longer than half a page; longer paragraphs are very hard to read.
- **M02.09 — Self-contained main text.** Do not make the main text depend on appendix exhibits. Give the takeaway and refer to the relevant appendix section; do not discuss the individual parts of appendix tables or figures in the main text.

## 3. The Introduction

Source: §3, [pages 2–3](original-memo.pdf#page=2).

### DOs

- **M03.01 — Priority.** Treat the introduction as the most important part of the paper; the memo says this is where authors win or lose editors and referees.
- **M03.02 — Standard introduction sequence.** Follow the standard introduction structure given in the memo.

| Order | Function | Extent specified in the memo |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook: a puzzle or problem | 1–2 paragraphs |
| 2 | Clear research question | 1 paragraph |
| 3 | Empirical approach and data | 1 paragraph |
| 4 | Detailed results | 3–4 paragraphs |
| 5 | Optional robustness summary | Useful for a job-market paper; can be very short for a journal submission |
| 6 | Value added relative to the literature | 1–3 paragraphs |
| 7 | Optional policy relevance and limitations | No paragraph count specified |
| 8 | Roadmap | 1 paragraph |

- **M03.03 — General interest.** Make the motivation intelligible beyond economics. The memo suggests asking a non-economist relative to explain why the question matters after reading the introduction; if they cannot, revise it.
- **M03.04 — Actual findings.** Tell readers what was found, not merely that there are findings.
- **M03.05 — Magnitudes.** Report the size of effects, not just their statistical significance.
- **M03.06 — Explicit contribution.** Make the contribution to existing literature crystal clear and state it explicitly.
- **M03.07 — Self-sufficiency.** The introduction alone should communicate the paper's work and findings sufficiently to understand what follows.
- **M03.08 — Effective motivation.** Motivate a question because it matters, is puzzling or controversial, or concerns something large or common. A gap in the literature can help, but is not sufficient motivation by itself.

### DON’Ts

- **M03.09 — No excess promises.** Treat the introduction as a contract: do not promise what the paper cannot deliver. Delivering less than promised costs credibility.
- **M03.10 — Conditional length benchmark.** Do not write an overly lengthy introduction. Be concise but informative while giving the full picture. An applied paper **without a model or structural estimation** should typically have an introduction no longer than **5–6 pages in a standard format**.
- **M03.11 — Integrated literature.** Do not add a standalone literature review section. Integrate the literature into the introduction, usually in 1–3 paragraphs, and cite other work in the body where results are compared.
- **M03.12 — No citation-list footnotes.** Avoid long footnotes whose only purpose is to say that many related papers exist. Explain the relationship.
- **M03.13 — Explicit author preference: conclusion citations.** Zhuravskaya prefers no citations in the conclusions. The memo explicitly labels this as her preference.

## 4. Titles and Abstracts

Source: §4, [pages 3–4](original-memo.pdf#page=3).

### DOs

- **M04.01 — Informative title.** Use a short, informative title. The memo notes that major contributions often join two key concepts directly, while follow-up papers can benefit from a subtitle.
- **M04.02 — Abstract length.** Make every word count; shorter is almost always better. Aim for a distilled **100–150-word abstract**, even when the journal does not require that limit. Reaching that range is hard but usually effective.
- **M04.03 — Complete abstract.** Include the question, approach, and key findings.

### DON’Ts

- **M04.04 — No empty appeal.** Do not choose an attention-grabbing but uninformative title.
- **M04.05 — Accessible references.** Avoid cultural references unless you are certain the broad audience will recognize them.
- **M04.06 — No abstract parentheses.** Do not use parentheses in the abstract.
- **M04.07 — Relationship in the title.** One-word titles rarely work, though exceptions exist. A title should usually convey a relationship between at least two objects.

## 5. Literature and Citations

Source: §5, [page 4](original-memo.pdf#page=4).

### DOs

- **M05.01 — Relevant coverage.** Cite all relevant papers.
- **M05.02 — Closest work.** Explain the contribution relative to the closest existing work.
- **M05.03 — Bibliography care.** Proofread and keep the reference list up to date; eliminate mistakes and typos. Authors notice when recent work is missing, and they are often the referees.
- **M05.04 — Citation syntax.** Use `\citet` and `\citep` correctly. Avoid double closing parentheses. Recognize that some sentences require a citation form without parenthesized years beyond those two forms.
- **M05.05 — Citation order.** Within a list of citations, order by publication year, then alphabetically within a year.
- **M05.06 — Dataset attribution.** Cite each data source at its first mention and include dataset references in the bibliography. The memo describes this as a data-availability rule for journals with data editors.

### DON’Ts

- **M05.07 — No undercitation.** Do not omit relevant work or select citations merely to favor likely referees. The memo warns that a thin citation list in a large literature signals ignorance or strategic gaming and can by itself cause a desk rejection.
- **M05.08 — No tangential overcitation.** Do not list many only loosely related strands of literature; this signals a scattered contribution.
- **M05.09 — Cite rather than review.** Discuss a paper at length only when it is central to the question. Explain differences from closely related work.
- **M05.10 — No separate literature section.** Integrate citations into the introduction; this repeats M03.11.
- **M05.11 — Use reviews when appropriate.** Cite literature reviews when a large literature would otherwise overcrowd the discussion.

## 6. Tables

Source: §6, [page 5](original-memo.pdf#page=5).

### DOs

- **M06.01 — Independent understanding.** Readers of only the text and readers of only the exhibits should each understand what was found.
- **M06.02 — One point per table.** Each table should be self-explanatory and make one distinct point.
- **M06.03 — Dependent variable first.** Put the dependent variable on the first line of the table and explicitly identify it as such. Giving it only in the caption is insufficient.
- **M06.04 — Readable labels.** Spell out column headers and variable names fully, using intuitive labels rather than programming names.
- **M06.05 — Outcome means.** Report the mean of the dependent variable for each column so readers can interpret magnitudes without additional calculation.
- **M06.06 — Observation unit.** State the unit of observation in the notes, especially when it differs between tables.
- **M06.07 — Column-specific information.** State the sample, estimation model, and other relevant specification information explicitly for every column.
- **M06.08 — Font size.** Do not use a font smaller than footnote size anywhere in an exhibit or its notes.
- **M06.09 — Informative rounding.** Report 2–3 decimal digits with two informative significant figures. The memo contrasts 0.095 with 0.1 and notes the meaningful difference between 0.059 and 0.095.
- **M06.10 — Matched precision.** Aim for the same number of digits in point estimates and standard errors, unless there is a reason to differ.
- **M06.11 — Standard error information.** Display enough digits for the reader to infer a standard error's value within **5 percent**.
- **M06.12 — Controls.** Display only the main coefficients, but list **all controls** in the notes and use indicators at the bottom to show which columns use which control sets.
- **M06.13 — Textual takeaway.** Explain what the reader should learn from each exhibit; do not assume the table explains its own relevance to the narrative.
- **M06.14 — Appendix exposition.** Discuss appendix exhibits in appendix text. The main text should report their takeaway and point to the appendix for details.

### DON’Ts

- **M06.15 — No abbreviated variable names.** Do not abbreviate dependent or explanatory variables so readers need the main text to decode them.
- **M06.16 — No illegible or dependent tables.** Do not use tiny fonts or require readers to consult the body to understand the exhibit. If readers must leave the table to learn what it shows, their attention is lost. This reinforces M06.01, M06.04, and M06.08.
- **M06.17 — No meaningless digits.** Do not report a point estimate such as 0.346789 alongside a standard error of 0.012.

## 7. Figures

Source: §7, [page 6](original-memo.pdf#page=6).

### DOs

- **M07.01 — Deliberate design.** Invest serious time in figures—the memo says to spend days thinking about them. A well-designed figure conveys a finding more immediately than any table.
- **M07.02 — Legend.** Always include a clear legend.
- **M07.03 — Figures and underlying tables.** Prefer figures for results when possible, with the underlying tables in the appendix.
- **M07.04 — Numerical estimates.** Print point estimates on coefficient plots, not only confidence intervals.

### DON’Ts

- **M07.05 — No pixelation.** Recheck sharpness after PDF compression; prefer vector formats.
- **M07.06 — Readable labels.** Use fonts and legends readable on paper or a small tablet, not only on a large monitor.
- **M07.07 — Final-size inspection.** Check fonts as they actually appear after figures have been scaled into the paper.

## 8. Ordering and Numbering of Exhibits

Source: §8, [page 6](original-memo.pdf#page=6).

### DOs

- **M08.01 — First-mention order.** Number tables, figures, and appendix sections consecutively in the order they first appear in the main text.
- **M08.02 — Start at one.** The first table cited must be Table 1; the first figure, Figure 1; the first appendix table, Table A.1; and the first appendix figure, Figure A.1. Continue with 2, 3, and so forth within each series.
- **M08.03 — Appendix letters.** Letter appendix sections A, B, C in their order of first mention in the text.

### DON’Ts

- **M08.04 — No late item first.** Do not make Table A6 or Appendix B the first reference in its series; this signals sloppiness.

## 9. Footnotes

Source: §9, [pages 6–7](original-memo.pdf#page=6).

### DOs

- **M09.01 — Density.** Use at most **one footnote per page on average** and at most **three on any single page**.
- **M09.02 — Placement.** In American English, put the marker after punctuation at the end of the sentence. The memo notes that British English has different formatting conventions.

### DON’Ts

- **M09.03 — No interruption.** Do not interrupt a sentence with a footnote. Read aloud with the note inserted at its marker to assess disruption.
- **M09.04 — Complete sentences.** Do not use fragments or lists as footnote text; use full sentences.

## 10. Language, Grammar, and Formulations

Source: §10, [pages 7–8](original-memo.pdf#page=7).

### DOs

- **M10.01 — Plural data.** Write “data are” and “data illustrate.”
- **M10.02 — Distinct dashes.** Use hyphens for compounds, en dashes for ranges, and em dashes for strong breaks, sparingly. The memo notes that language models tend to overuse em dashes. These are the memo's American-English conventions; it notes that British conventions differ.
- **M10.03 — Full forms.** Spell out contractions in formal writing.
- **M10.04 — Authorial voice.** Prefer “the paper shows” to “I show” in most contexts. The memo's stated reason is a softer, more humble tone.
- **M10.05 — Natural phrasing.** If a formulation is awkward when read aloud, do not use it in writing.
- **M10.06 — Acronyms.** Keep acronyms to a bare minimum and, whenever possible, spell terms out. Use none in introductions or conclusions. Define any acronym elsewhere on first use.
- **M10.07 — Theory notation.** Remind readers what notation means when stating key theoretical results so they do not need to memorize every symbol to read the paper.

### DON’Ts

- **M10.08 — Unnecessary first person.** Avoid “I” when it is not clearly needed. Almost never use “my data,” “our data,” “my strategy,” or “our strategy,” because most of the time the data are not yours; avoid “my analysis,” which the memo describes as usually awkward.
- **M10.09 — Single authorship.** Do not use a royal “we” in a single-authored paper.
- **M10.10 — Parenthetical compression.** Do not use parentheses in the main text. In particular, never use constructions like “increase (decrease)” or pair two groups and two results through parentheses.
- **M10.11 — No slash conjunctions.** Do not use a slash as a conjunction, as in “firms/households” or “urban/rural”; write out the relationship.
- **M10.12 — Repeated grammar cautions.** Do not use contractions or “data is”; this repeats M10.01 and M10.03. The memo notes that language models systematically mishandle “data are.”
- **M10.13 — Empirical interpretation in words.** Do not discuss empirical findings merely as a coefficient symbol being positive and significant. Name the effect it measures and describe that result directly.

## 11. English and Copyediting

Source: §11, [page 8](original-memo.pdf#page=8).

### DOs

- **M11.01 — Copyediting.** Invest in a good copy editor, human or AI-assisted. Language models are useful for grammar and sentence-level flow.
- **M11.02 — Avoid AI habits.** Prompt against recognizable AI prose habits, including “delve,” “illuminate,” “enduring,” and excessive em dashes.
- **M11.03 — Evaluate changes.** Always critically assess every proposed change, whether from a human or an AI.
- **M11.04 — Visible edits.** Always ask for changes to be highlighted and understand the logic behind each one.
- **M11.05 — Consistency checks.** Consider AI consistency-checking tools for consistency across theory, empirical methodology, tables, and their discussion in the main text and appendix.
- **M11.06 — Source-specific tool suggestion.** The author mentions finding Refine.ink useful and reports an approximate price of $40 per paper, while disclosing no relationship or stake. She explicitly welcomes other ways of checking a draft.

### DON’Ts

- **M11.07 — Copyediting is not research validation.** Do not overestimate a copy editor's ability to correct argument structure or research-level errors, especially an AI copy editor's.
- **M11.08 — Review generated prose.** Do not leave AI-written prose without careful review; language models are often wrong.

## 12. Rewriting and Revision

Source: §12, [page 8](original-memo.pdf#page=8). The original does not divide this final section into DOs and DON’Ts.

- **M12.01 — Revision as habit.** Rereading and rewriting should be the main writing habit; proofread continually.
- **M12.02 — Listen.** Read aloud, record, and listen. If it sounds awkward, it is awkward.
- **M12.03 — Cut and clarify.** Apply both throughout revision.
- **M12.04 — Willingness to rewrite.** Do not treat existing text as sacred; sometimes rewrite it fully.
- **M12.05 — Respond to misunderstanding.** If referees get the paper all wrong, it means the paper was explained badly; rewrite more clearly.
- **M12.06 — Priority of writing.** Never treat poor writing as a minor issue; it is a major problem.
